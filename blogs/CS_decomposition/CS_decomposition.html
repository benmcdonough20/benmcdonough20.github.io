Angles between subspaces
Blog Post 2-10-25

Introduction

It is often desirable to measure the difference between two subspaces. The case where this often arises is in perturbation theory. For instance, let \(H\) be Hermitian, and consider \(H \oplus (1 + \epsilon Z)\) and \(H \oplus (1 + \epsilon X)\). As \(\epsilon \to 0\), it is clear that the eigenvalues of both matrices approach each other, but what about the eigenvectors? In the first case, the new eigenvectors are \(\ket{0}, \ket{1}\), and in the second case they are \(\ket{+}, \ket{-}\) for any finite value of \(\epsilon\). The key realization is that the subspaces they span will become closer and closer. We can characterize the separation of 1D subspaces by an angle \(\theta\), and this idea is generalized to multi-dimensional subspaces through the notion of principal angles.

Principal angles and the SVD

Def 1. Given two (finite-dimensional) subspaces \(F\) and \(G\), let \(\cos(\theta_1) = \langle \vec u, \vec v\rangle\), where \(\mathbb B\) is the unit ball. Since \(\mathbb B\) is compact in finite dimensions, we can choose \(u_1, v_1\) that achieve the maximal \(\theta_1\). With \(\{u_1, \dots, u_k\}\) and \(\{v_1, \dots, v_k\}\) constructed, we choose \(u_{k+1}, v_{k+1}\) to maximize \(\cos(\theta_k)\) subject to the constraint \(\langle u_{k+1}, u_{i \leq k}\rangle = \langle v_{k+1}, v_{i \leq k}\rangle = 0\). The vectors \(\{u_1, \dots, u_n\}\) and \(\{v_1, \dots, v_n\}\) are called principal vectors, and \(\{\theta_1, \dots, \theta_k\}\) are called principal angles.

Remark 1. While the principal vectors are not unique, the principal angles are.

Def 2. Let \(M\) be a matrix. \(\Vert M \Vert_2 = \max_{\vec v \in \mathbb B}\Vert M \vec v \Vert_2\) is called the Schatten \(2-\)norm.

Prop 1. \(\Vert M \Vert_2\) is given by \(\Vert M \Vert_2 = \max_{y \in \mathbb B}\max_{x \in \mathbb B}\langle y, Mx\rangle\). This follows from the singular value decomposition.

Prop 2. Let \(P_F\) project onto \(F\) and \(P_G\) project onto \(G\). Then the canonical angles are given by the singular values of \(P_F^\dagger P_G\).

Proof. Simply note that the largest singular value is \[\max_{y \in \mathbb B}\max_{x \in \mathbb B}\langle P_F y, P_G x\rangle = \max_{y \in \mathbb B \cap F}\max_{x \in \mathbb B \cap G} \langle y,x\rangle = \theta_1\] where \(\theta_1\) is the largest principal angle. The iteration to the subsequent singular values is the same as the one used to find the principal angles. ◻

The CS decomposition

Prop 3. If \(A\) is a submatrix of \(M\), then the singular values of \(A\) are bounded by the maximum singular value of \(M\).

Proof. Put \[M = \begin{pmatrix}A & B \\ C & D\end{pmatrix}\] The maximum singular value of \(M\) is given by \(\Vert M \Vert_2\), which follows from the singular value decomposition. Then \[\begin{aligned} \Vert M \Vert_2^2 \geq \Vert M \vec v\Vert_2^2 = \Vert A \vec v \Vert_2^2 + \Vert C \vec v \Vert_2^2 \geq \Vert A \vec v \Vert_2^2 \end{aligned}\] Taking the supremum on both sides and using the definition \(\Vert A \Vert_2 = \sup_{\vec v \in \mathbb B}\Vert A\vec v \Vert\) proves the claim. ◻

Theorem 1 (CS Decomposition (thin)). Let \(M\) be a \(2k \times 2k\) matrix with orthonormal columns. Then there exists a unitary \(U = \operatorname{diag}(U_1, U_2)\) and \(V\) such that \[\begin{aligned} U^\dagger M V &= \begin{pmatrix}C \\ S\end{pmatrix} \end{aligned}\] where \(C = \operatorname{diag}(\cos(\theta_1), \dots, \cos(\theta_k))\) and \(S = \operatorname{diag}(\sin(\theta_1), \dots, \sin(\theta_k))\) for some angles \(0 \leq \theta_1, \dots, \theta_k \leq 1\).

Proof. Let \(M = \begin{pmatrix}X \\ Y\end{pmatrix}\). Choose unitaries \(U_1, V\) such that \(U_1^\dagger X V = C\), where \(C = \operatorname{diag}(c_1, \dots, c_k)\). Since \(X\) is a submatrix of \(M\) whose singular values are all 1, we have \(c_i \leq 1\). Then \[\begin{pmatrix}U_1^\dagger & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}X \\ Y\end{pmatrix}V = \begin{pmatrix}U_1^\dagger X V \\ YV\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}C \\ YV\end{pmatrix}\] This transformation preserves the orthonormality of the columns of \(M\). We have \(M^\dagger M = C^2 + (YV)^\dagger (YV) = 1\). This implies that the columns of \(YV\) are orthogonal, and their norm is \(\Vert y_i \Vert^2 = 1-c_i^2\). Thus we can find a unitary matrix \(Q\) so that \(Q^\dagger YV = S\), where \(S = \operatorname{diag}(1-c_1^2, \dots, 1-c_k^2)\). Therefore \[\begin{pmatrix}U_1^\dagger & 0 \\ 0 & Q^\dagger\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}X \\ Y\end{pmatrix}V = \begin{pmatrix}U_1^\dagger X V \\ QYV\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}C \\ S\end{pmatrix}\] This completes the proof. ◻

Theorem 2 (CS decomposition). Let \[\begin{aligned} M = \begin{bmatrix}X & Y \\ Z & W\end{bmatrix} \end{aligned}\] be a unitary matrix with \(k \times k\) blocks \(X, Y, Z, W\). Then there exist unitaries \(U = \operatorname{diag}(U_1, U_2)\), \(V = \operatorname{diag}(V_1, V_2)\) such that \[U^\dagger M V = \begin{pmatrix}C & -S \\ S & C\end{pmatrix}\] where \(C = \operatorname{diag}(\cos(\theta_1), \dots, \cos(\theta_k))\) and \(S = \operatorname{diag}(\sin(\theta_1), \dots, \sin(\theta_k))\) with angles \(\theta_1, \dots, \theta_k \leq \frac{\pi}{2}\).

Proof. First, choose \(U_1, V_1\) so that \(U_1^\dagger X V_1 = C\). Since \(X\) is a submatrix of \(M\) whose singular values are all 1, we have \(C = \operatorname{diag}(c_1, \dots, c_k)\) where \(0 \leq c_i \leq 1\). Then we have \[\begin{pmatrix}U_1^\dagger & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}X & Y \\ Z & W\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}V_1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}U_1^\dagger X V_1 & U_1^\dagger Y \\ ZV_1 & W\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}C & Y' \\ Z' & W\end{pmatrix}\] This transformation preserves the orthonormality of the rows and columns. Therefore \(\langle \vec z'_i, \vec z'_j \rangle = \delta_{ij}(1-c_i^2)\). This same equation is satisfied by the rows of \(Y'\). Therefore we can find unitaries \(R\) and \(L\) such that \(L^\dagger Z' = T\) and \(Y'R = -S\), where \(S = \operatorname{diag}(1-c_1^2, \dots, 1-c_k^2)\). As such,

\[\begin{pmatrix}U_1^\dagger & 0 \\ 0 & L^\dagger\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}X & Y \\ Z & W\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}V_1 & 0 \\ 0 & R\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}U_1^\dagger X V_1 & U^\dagger_1YR \\ L^\dagger ZV_1 & L^\dagger WR\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}C & S \\ -S & W'\end{pmatrix}\] Writing \(C = 1 \oplus C'\), where \(C'\) consists of \(c_i\) strictly less than 1, then we have \[\begin{pmatrix}C & S \\ -S & W'\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}\mqty{1 & 0 \\ 0 & C'} & \mqty{0 & 0 \\ 0 & S'} \\ \mqty{0 & 0 \\ 0 & -S'} & \mqty{X_1 & X_2 \\ X_3 & X_4}\end{pmatrix}\] Taking the inner product of the last row with the second row gives \(S'X_4^\dagger-S'C' = 0\), where the unitarity of \(M\) guarantees that the inner product of rows is zero. Since \(S'\) is invertible (because \(1-c_i^2 > 0\)), we have \(X_4 = C'\), and by the orthonormality of the rows, \(X_2 = X_3 = 0\). This then implies that \(X_1 = 1\) by the same logic. Therefore we have \[\begin{pmatrix}\mqty{1 & 0 \\ 0 & C'} & \mqty{0 & 0 \\ 0 & S'} \\ \mqty{0 & 0 \\ 0 & -S'} & \mqty{X_1 & X_2 \\ X_3 & X_4}\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}\mqty{1 & 0 \\ 0 & C'} & \mqty{0 & 0 \\ 0 & S'} \\ \mqty{0 & 0 \\ 0 & -S'} & \mqty{1 & 0 \\ 0 & C'}\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}C & -S \\ S & C\end{pmatrix}\] Since \(0 \leq c_i \leq 1\), we can put \(c_i \equiv \cos(\theta_i)\) for some \(0 \leq \theta \leq \pi/2\), and \(s_i = 1-c_i^2 = \sin(\theta_i)\). This completes the proof. ◻

Halmos two-projections theorem

Theorem 3 (Halmos two-projection theorem (finite dimensions)). Suppose that \(F, G \leq V\) are finite-dimensional subspaces of equal dimensions \(k\) with \(\dim V = 2k\) and \(P_F, P_G\) are projectors onto these spaces. Put \[P(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix}\cos^{2}(\theta) & \sin(\theta)\cos(\theta) \\ \sin(\theta)\cos(\theta) & \sin^{2}(\theta)\end{pmatrix}\] and \[P_0 = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0\end{pmatrix}\] then there are angles \(\theta_1, \dots, \theta_k\) and a basis in which \[\begin{aligned} P_F &= \begin{pmatrix}P_0 & 0 & \dots & 0 \\ 0 & P_0 & \dots & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \dots & P_0\end{pmatrix} \\ P_G &= \begin{pmatrix}P(\theta_1) & 0 & \dots & 0\\ 0 & P(\theta_2) & \dots & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \dots & P(\theta_k)\end{pmatrix} \end{aligned}\]

Proof. Let \(X,X_\perp, Y, Y_{\perp}\) be matrices whose columns form a basis for \(F,F^\perp, G, G^\perp\) respectively. Then \((X, X_\perp)\) is unitary. Thus \(M = (X, X_\perp)^\dagger Y\) has orthogonal columns. Therefore we can find \(U_1, U_2, V\) such that \[U^\dagger M V = \begin{pmatrix}U_1^\dagger & 0 \\ 0 & U_2^\dagger\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}X^\dagger Y \\ X_\perp^\dagger Y\end{pmatrix}V = \begin{pmatrix}U_1^\dagger X^\dagger YV \\ U_2^\dagger X_\perp^\dagger YV\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}C \\ S\end{pmatrix}\] Since \(P_F = XX^\dagger\) and \(P_G = YY^\dagger\), putting \(U = (XU_1, X_{\perp} U_2)\), we have \[U^\dagger P_F U = \begin{pmatrix}U_1^\dagger & 0 \\ 0 & U_2^\dagger\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}U_1^\dagger & 0 \\ 0 & U_2^\dagger\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0\end{pmatrix}\] \[U^\dagger P_G U = (U^\dagger Y V)(V^\dagger Y^\dagger U) = \begin{pmatrix}C \\ S\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}C & S\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}C^2 & CS \\ CS & S^2\end{pmatrix}\] Row and column operations corresponding to unitary matrices can then be used to bring this into the desired form. ◻

There is also a slick proof using group representation theory:

Proof using group representation theory. Let \(P_F, P_G\) be as before. Put \(R_F = 1 - 2P_F\) and \(R_G = 1-2P_G\) (these are reflections about the given subspaces). One can check that these are unitary operators. Putting \(s = R_F\) and \(r = R_FR_G\), we can see that \(s^2 = (1-2P_F)(1-2P_F) = 1-4P_F + 4P_F^2 = 1\) and \[srs = R_F^2R_G R_F = R_GR_F = r^{-1}\] Thus, the group \(G\) generated by \(R_F, R_G\) is an infinite dihedral group, and \(\langle r \rangle\) is a normal abelian subgroup.

Now we classify the irreducible representations \(V\) of \(G\). Let \(N\) be the group generated by \(r\). There are two cosets in \(G/N\), represented by \(e\) and \(s\). Let \(W \leq V\) be an irreducible representation of \(N\). Then \(W + sW\) is an invariant subspace of \(V\), so \(V = W + sW\). By Shur’s lemma, since \(N\) is Abelian, \(W\) is one-dimensional. This classifies all the irreps, and also gives us a way to construct them. We must have \(r \mapsto e^{i\lambda}\) for some \(\lambda\). Therefore \(V\) is spanned by \(v, sv\), and since \(s^2 = 1\), we have \(s \mapsto \begin{pmatrix}0 & 1\\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}\) in this basis. Since \(rs = sr^{-1}\), we have \(r = \operatorname{diag}(e^{i\lambda}, e^{-i\lambda})\), where \(\lambda < \pi\) (this is because \(\lambda > \pi\) is clearly isomorphic to a representation with \(\lambda < \pi\)). A basis transformation brings these operators into the form \[\begin{aligned} s &= \begin{pmatrix}-1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix} \\ r &= \begin{pmatrix}\cos(\lambda) & -\sin(\lambda) \\ \sin(\lambda) & \cos(\lambda)\end{pmatrix} \end{aligned}\] Since all unitary representations are completely reducible, we can decompose \(R_F, R_G\) into a block-diagonal form with blocks resembling the above. Then the blocks of the projectors are given by \[P_F = \frac{1-s}{2} = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0\end{pmatrix}\] \[P_G = \frac{1-sr}{2} = \begin{pmatrix}\cos^{2}(\lambda/2) & \cos(\lambda/2)\sin(\lambda/2) \\ \cos(\lambda/2)\sin(\lambda/2) & \sin^{2}(\lambda/2)\end{pmatrix}\] Since we can take \(\lambda/2 < \pi/2\), the conclusion is the same. ◻

Prop 4. The angles in the two-projections theorem are the principal angles between the subspaces.

Proof. Consider \[P_F^\dagger P_G \sim \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}C^2 & CS \\ CS & S^2\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}C^2 & CS \\ 0 & 0\end{pmatrix}\] Then the singular values of the above matrix are the square roots of the eigenvalues of \[\begin{pmatrix}C^2 & CS \\ 0 & 0\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}C^2 & CS \\ 0 & 0\end{pmatrix}^\dagger = \begin{pmatrix}C^2(C^2+S^2) & 0 \\ 0 & 0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}C^2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0\end{pmatrix}\] Therefore the eigenvalues are just the diagonal entries of \(C\), which are \(\cos(\theta_1), \dots, \cos(\theta_k)\). ◻

Connection to direct rotations

Note that the projectors \(P_0\) and \(P(\theta)\) are exactly the projection operators we would get if \(F\) and \(G\) were two dimensional subspaces spanned by \(v_1\) and \(\cos(\theta)v_1 + \sin(\theta)v_2\). This is made even clearer by the group-theoretic proof; the images of \(s\) and \(r\) in the irreps of the infinite dihedral group are just an Euler rotation matrix and a reflection. The action of \(G\) is therefore always expressible as a action on a direct sum of two-dimensional subspaces. This gives a simple description of direct rotations between subspaces. Since a direct rotation between two 1D subspaces is always given by an Euler rotation matrix, a direct rotation more generally can be written as a sum of these direct rotations between two subspaces.
